A shop drawing is a fabrication-level drawing produced by (or for) the contractor, fabricator, or installer — it shows exact dimensions, materials, hardware, and assembly details needed to actually build or install something like millwork, casework, or tile and stone work. Construction documents, by contrast, are the architect's design-intent drawings used for permitting and bidding.

Shop drawings translate that design intent into build-ready instructions for the shop floor and job site, which is where our "shop-floor logic" approach comes in.

At Ethos Drafting Studio, we provide detailed architectural shop drawings, millwork and casework shop drawings, tile and stone shop drawings, fabrication drawings, and installation documentation based on project requirements, design documents, specifications, and field information.

A comprehensive millwork shop drawing package may include:

  • Plans, Elevations & Sections — Scaled plan views, interior and exterior elevations, sectional views, enlarged details, and key references showing the complete configuration and relationship to adjacent construction.
  • Detailed Dimensions & Critical Clearances — Overall dimensions, component dimensions, opening sizes, finished dimensions, field dimensions, installation clearances, appliance clearances, and required tolerances.
  • Cabinet & Casework Construction — Cabinet construction type, box configurations, partitions, adjustable shelves, fixed shelves, backs, bottoms, tops, stretchers, drawer construction, door construction, and internal components.
  • Joinery & Fabrication Details — Detailed information for dados, rabbets, mortise-and-tenon conditions, dowel connections, spline joints, concealed fasteners, mechanical fasteners, blocking, cleats, and other specified joinery methods.
  • Materials & Substrate Specifications — Wood species, hardwood, plywood, MDF, particleboard, HPL, veneer, solid surface, decorative laminates, metal components, substrate thicknesses, and other specified materials.
  • Veneer & Finish Information — Veneer species and cut, grain direction, sequence matching, slip matching, book matching, end matching, finish system, sheen, stain, paint, laminate orientation, edge treatment, and exposed-edge conditions.
  • Door, Drawer & Hardware Schedule — Door and drawer sizes, reveals, overlay or inset conditions, hinge locations, drawer slides, pulls, locks, catches, specialty hardware, and hardware references.
  • Profiles, Reveals & Architectural Details — Base and toe-kick conditions, scribes, fillers, edge profiles, moldings, trim returns, shadow gaps, reveals, panel joints, end conditions, transitions, and interface details.
  • Countertops & Integrated Components — Countertop construction, edge profiles, seams, backsplashes, cutouts, sink integration, appliance interfaces, support conditions, and substrate requirements where applicable.
  • Anchoring & Installation Details — Wall cleats, blocking requirements, brackets, support framing, anchorage, fasteners, leveling requirements, field assembly, and attachment to the building structure or substrate.
  • Architectural & MEP Coordination — Coordination with walls, ceilings, flooring, doors, glazing, electrical devices, lighting, plumbing, HVAC, appliances, and other adjacent trades where required.
  • Panel & Part Identification — Part numbers, cabinet IDs, elevation references, sheet references, material tags, assembly references, and fabrication or installation callouts for efficient shop and field coordination.
  • Tolerances & Field Verification — Critical field dimensions, site verification requirements, allowable tolerances, scribing conditions, out-of-plumb or out-of-level conditions, and other information necessary for accurate installation.
  • Revision & Approval Control — Drawing numbers, revision clouds, revision descriptions, approval status, date, detail references, and other document-control information required throughout the submittal process.

A properly developed millwork shop drawing does more than represent the finished appearance. It establishes the technical relationship between design intent, material selection, construction methodology, fabrication, field conditions, and installation requirements.

Before a shop drawing package is submitted for review, it should be checked for dimensional accuracy, coordination, constructability, material information, and compliance with the project documents. A typical architectural shop drawing checklist includes:

  • Project & Drawing Information — Project name and number, drawing title, sheet number, detail references, scale, date, revision number, and applicable specification sections.
  • Design Document Coordination — Verify that the shop drawings correspond with the latest architectural drawings, elevations, sections, reflected ceiling plans, finish plans, schedules, and project specifications.
  • Dimensions & Field Conditions — Check overall dimensions, component dimensions, opening sizes, clearances, finished dimensions, field-verified dimensions, tolerances, and existing site conditions.
  • Plans, Elevations & Sections — Confirm that all required plan views, interior and exterior elevations, building sections, enlarged plans, wall sections, and construction details are clearly referenced and coordinated.
  • Material & Finish Schedule — Identify substrates, material types, thicknesses, species, colors, finishes, laminates, veneers, edge treatments, profiles, and manufacturer references where applicable.
  • Assembly & Fabrication Details — Review component breakdowns, assembly conditions, joints, connections, fastening methods, backing, blocking, supports, welds, anchors, and other fabrication-specific details.
  • Hardware & Accessory Coordination — Verify hardware types, sizes, locations, mounting conditions, hinges, slides, locks, pulls, brackets, accessories, and specialty hardware.
  • Interface & Trade Coordination — Check interfaces with structural elements, walls, ceilings, flooring, doors, glazing, electrical devices, plumbing fixtures, HVAC components, lighting, appliances, and adjacent trades.
  • Installation Requirements — Confirm anchorage, attachment methods, support conditions, installation clearances, sequencing requirements, leveling, shimming, scribing, and field-assembly conditions.
  • Code & Specification Requirements — Review applicable project specifications, approved materials, required performance criteria, accessibility requirements, fire-rated conditions, and other project-specific requirements.
  • Document Control & Revisions — Ensure revision clouds, revision descriptions, drawing status, approval stamps, RFI references, submittal numbers, and other document-control information are current.
  • Quality Control Review — Perform a final constructability and coordination review to identify dimensional conflicts, missing details, inconsistent annotations, unresolved interfaces, or discrepancies before submission.

The cost of millwork shop drawings depends on the project scope, level of detailing, fabrication requirements, drawing volume, coordination requirements, and turnaround time.

As a general budgeting guideline, millwork shop drawing services may be estimated at approximately 5% of the total millwork contract value. This is a rule of thumb rather than a fixed industry rate, and the actual drafting fee can vary depending on project complexity and documentation requirements.

$50,000 Millwork Contract × 5% = approximately $2,500 for shop drawings

A higher or lower percentage may be appropriate depending on factors such as:

  • Custom fabrication and complex joinery
  • Number of elevations, sections, and enlarged details
  • Cabinet and casework quantity
  • Veneer sequencing and finish requirements
  • Hardware and specialty component coordination
  • Field verification and existing-condition documentation
  • Architectural, structural, and MEP coordination
  • Revisions and submittal cycles
  • Tight or expedited project schedules

At Ethos Drafting Studio, we use the project scope and required level of technical documentation to establish the appropriate fee. For larger or clearly defined packages, we can provide a fixed project quotation.

AutoCAD and Revit are both widely used for producing shop drawings, fabrication drawings, and technical construction documentation. The appropriate platform depends on the project scope, level of detail, coordination requirements, and the client's existing documentation workflow.

AutoCAD for Shop Drawings

AutoCAD remains highly effective for detailed 2D shop drawing production, particularly for fabrication-intensive scopes where precise linework, dimensions, sections, profiles, and construction details are required. For millwork and casework shop drawings, AutoCAD allows detailed development of cabinet layouts, elevations, sections, joinery, edge profiles, reveals, fillers, substrates, hardware locations, and installation details.

Revit for Shop Drawings

Revit uses a BIM-based, model-driven workflow, making it suitable for projects where shop drawing documentation needs to remain coordinated with a three-dimensional building model. Revit workflows can support parametric modeling, component-based documentation, BIM coordination, material data, model-based views, and drawing extraction.

Which Should You Use?

The choice between AutoCAD and Revit for shop drawings should be based on the project's technical requirements rather than software preference alone. For some projects, a combined AutoCAD and Revit workflow can be practical — using Revit for BIM coordination and model development while using AutoCAD for specific 2D fabrication details and trade documentation.

At Ethos Drafting Studio, we use AutoCAD and Revit according to the project's scope, documentation standards, and deliverable requirements. We can work from architectural drawings, design development documents, PDFs, sketches, CAD backgrounds, Revit models, specifications, and field information.

White-label drafting is a professional drafting service where a specialized drafting company prepares drawings and technical documentation on behalf of another architecture, design, construction, millwork, or fabrication company, while the client-facing company retains the relationship and presents the deliverables under its own brand.

How Does White-Label Drafting Work?
  • Project Information — The client provides architectural drawings, design development documents, CAD files, Revit models, specifications, sketches, markups, field dimensions, or other relevant project information.
  • Technical Drafting & Documentation — The drafting team develops the required shop drawings, fabrication drawings, architectural details, elevations, sections, layouts, schedules, or installation documentation according to the client's standards.
  • Client Standards & Branding — Drawings can follow the client's established title blocks, layer standards, annotation styles, sheet numbering, naming conventions, detail standards, and document-control procedures.
  • Quality Control — Completed drawings can undergo an independent technical review before delivery to help identify dimensional discrepancies, coordination issues, missing information, annotation inconsistencies, and fabrication or installation conflicts.
  • Client Delivery — The completed documentation is returned to the client for their internal review, submittal, fabrication, construction, or installation workflow.
Why Do Companies Use White-Label Drafting Services?

White-label drafting allows companies to increase drafting capacity, manage workload fluctuations, meet project deadlines, and access specialized technical drafting resources without maintaining a large permanent drafting department. It can be particularly useful for architecture and interior design firms, general contractors and subcontractors, millwork and casework manufacturers, cabinet and architectural millwork companies, tile and stone contractors, fabricators and specialty trades, and construction documentation teams.

Ethos Drafting Studio provides white-label architectural drafting, millwork shop drawings, casework documentation, tile and stone shop drawings, fabrication drawings, CAD documentation, Revit/BIM support, and technical detailing for professional AEC and specialty-trade companies.

A professional terrazzo shop drawing should clearly communicate the complete layout and technical requirements needed for coordination, fabrication, and installation. Depending on the project and terrazzo system, the drawing package can include floor plans, enlarged layouts, elevations, sections, setting-out dimensions, terrazzo patterns, color and material references, divider strips, expansion and control-joint locations, transitions, stair treads and risers, edge conditions, floor-level changes, and attachment details.

For projects with custom designs, the drawings can also document decorative borders, logos, geometric patterns, inlays, aggregate/color zones, centerlines, alignment points, and piece identification. NTMA's guide specifications specifically identify plans, elevations, sections, component details, attachments to adjacent work, installation requirements, divider strips, expansion-joint strips, accessory strips, abrasive strips, stairs, and terrazzo patterns as shop-drawing information where applicable.

At Ethos Drafting Studio, we organize this information into coordinated CAD/BIM documentation so that the terrazzo layout can be checked against architectural backgrounds, flooring transitions, walls, columns, doors, stairs, millwork, floor drains, and adjacent finish materials before installation.

For U.S. projects, terrazzo shop drawings can be developed with reference to the applicable National Terrazzo & Mosaic Association (NTMA) technical specifications and guide details, together with the project's construction specifications, selected terrazzo system, manufacturer requirements, and applicable codes and standards.

NTMA publishes technical specifications for systems including epoxy, bonded, monolithic, sand-cushion, polyacrylate, and rustic terrazzo. The applicable requirements can significantly change depending on the terrazzo system. For example, NTMA's technical information identifies different thicknesses and construction characteristics for epoxy, monolithic, bonded, and other systems.

Joint and divider coordination is also an important part of the documentation. NTMA provides specific guide details for contraction joints, isolation joints, control joints, construction joints, divider strips, transitions to other flooring, ramps, and expansion-joint systems.

At Ethos, we therefore do not apply one generic "terrazzo standard" to every project. We develop the drawings around the specified terrazzo system and project requirements, using applicable industry references as the technical basis for the documentation.
